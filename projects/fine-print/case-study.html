Borrow

Fine Print

What a loan or an instalment plan really costs a year, and which offer is cheaper.

Open Fine Print

Problem

Instalment plans and small loans are usually explained by a monthly fee or a small payment, not by what they cost in a year. “0.6% a month” sounds like 7.2% a year, but because the amount is paid back as you go, you only have about half of it on average, and the real yearly rate is 13%. A loan of 500 that costs 75 over 14 days is 391% a year. When two offers are described in different ways, most people cannot tell which one is cheaper, and the person selling the plan is rarely the one who explains it.

What it does

  • Takes an offer the way it is usually told: the same payment each time, a monthly fee on the amount, a yearly interest rate, or one payment later. Fees at the start are included.
  • Gives one comparable number, the real yearly rate, with what you get, what you pay back and what it costs.
  • Explains the gap in one sentence, using the numbers that were entered.
  • Puts up to three offers side by side, marks the lowest rate, and says when the lowest total cost is a different offer.
  • Shows every payment split into cost and repayment, and copies a short summary to send to family before signing.
  • English and Chinese, any currency symbol. It works offline, and nothing typed leaves the device.

Examples

The offer as describedReal yearly rate
12,000 in 12 instalments, 0.6% monthly fee13.0%
The same with a 1% monthly fee21.5%
10,000 at 8% a year over 24 months, 300 fee at the start11.0%
Get 500, pay back 575 after 14 days391%

How the rate is worked out

The page finds the rate per payment period at which all the payments, valued today, add up to exactly the money received: the internal rate of return, the same basis lenders use for an APR. That rate is multiplied by the number of periods in a year; the compounded figure is shown under “How is the real rate worked out?”. A plain 8% loan with no fees comes out at exactly 8.00%, which is how I checked the maths.

Design

The result is laid out like a receipt, with dotted leaders and fixed-width figures, because that is how people already read what they owe. The colours are paper and ink, with one seal red for the cost and for the “Lowest rate” stamp. On a phone, the rate rides along at the bottom of the screen while you type, so you see it change without scrolling.

Next Planned

  • Add the forms that confuse people most after these: deferred interest, and a large final payment.
  • Share an offer as a link, so a family member opens the same numbers.
