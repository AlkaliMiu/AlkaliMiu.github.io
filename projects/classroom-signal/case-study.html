Project 1 · Dashboard and map

Classroom Signal

A dashboard that shows which schools in a region have usable internet, built for people who often work on a phone over a slow connection.

Open the live demo
Role
Design and front end, on my own

1The problem

When a government decides to connect every school, the people who follow up are district education officers, regulators and internet providers. They need to answer three questions quickly:

  1. How many schools reach the target speed?
  2. Where are the problems?
  3. What is wrong at one particular school, and who should fix it?

Many of these users work from a phone, on 3G or a shared connection, and are not data specialists. A heavy GIS tool with map tiles and web fonts can take a minute to load for them, or fail completely.

2Design decisions

DecisionWhy
The page opens with a sentence (“27 of 128 schools reach 20 Mbps”), not a chartIt answers the first question for any reader in two seconds, and a screen reader reads it first.
Each status has its own shape, colour and words (circle, triangle, square, hollow circle)Colour alone fails for colour-blind users and on cheap or sunlit screens (WCAG 1.4.1).
The map is drawn on graph paper; each large square is 5 kmThere are no map tiles to download, the page still shows distance, and the look fits a school context.
The school list is the main path for keyboard and screen-reader usersA map with 128 tab stops is unusable; the list has the same information, with search and sorting. The map has a written summary.
One HTML file, about 60 KB (about 19 KB compressed), with system fonts and no network requestsIt opens on a slow connection and keeps working offline once loaded.
On phones the page shows a List / Map switch, and every control is at least 40 px tallThumbs need large targets, and a small screen cannot show both views well.
English and French, with French typography (non-breaking spaces, Mbit/s)Many target countries work in French. Switching updates the page language for screen readers too.
“Copy list” copies the current schools as CSVOfficers paste lists into spreadsheets, emails and messaging apps; it fits how they already work.
The target speed can be changed (10, 20 or 50 Mbps)Targets differ between countries and change over time.
Colours, type sizes and controls come from a shared design systemThe same tokens and status shapes are used in Signal Setup, so a school is described the same way on both screens.

3Accessibility checks (WCAG 2.2 AA)

  • Text contrast is at least 4.99:1 in light mode; control borders are at least 3.49:1.
  • Visible focus outline on every control, a skip link, and Escape to close a school’s details.
  • The daily speed chart has a table version under “Show daily values”.
  • Filter changes are announced through a live region (“23 schools shown”).
  • Animation is turned off when the system asks for reduced motion.
  • Light and dark themes follow the device setting.

Audit, 10 October 2026

I ran axe-core 4.14 (the WCAG 2.0 to 2.2 A and AA rules, plus best practices) on 26 states of the dashboard. They cover light and dark themes, phone and desktop widths, English and French, a school’s details with the daily table open, a zoomed district, an empty search and the map view on phones. The first run found two problems:

  • Label in Name (WCAG 2.5.3). Each school row had a hidden label that left out words shown on screen, such as the connection type and “7-day median”. The language buttons showed “EN” and “FR” but were named “English” and “Français”. People using voice control say the words they see, so the name has to contain them. The rows now take their name from their visible text, plus hidden status words, and the buttons are named “EN, English” and “FR, Français”.
  • No main landmark. Screen-reader users could not jump straight to the content. The page now has one.

While fixing these, I also made the skip link always land on something visible: the open school, or the school list (switching from the map on phones). After the fixes, axe reports 0 violations in all 26 states.

axe could not measure contrast for text drawn inside the SVG map and chart, or for list rows scrolled out of view. I checked those colour pairs from the tokens instead: map labels are 7.84:1 (light) and 8.17:1 (dark), and chart labels 5.37:1 and 5.66:1.

Lighthouse 12.8 (phone emulation with a slow 4G connection and a 4× slower processor) scores the dashboard 100 for performance, accessibility, best practices and SEO, with the largest content drawn in 1.2 seconds. Best practices was 96 at first, because the browser asked for a missing favicon.ico; the icon is now inline, so no request is made.

By hand, I checked keyboard-only use (skip link, list, details, Escape back to the same row), the WCAG 1.4.12 text-spacing test at 375 px, and reflow at 320 px. I have not yet tested with screen readers such as NVDA, TalkBack or VoiceOver, or with people who use them; that is the next check.

4What I would do next

  • Test the flow with three or four district officers and watch where they hesitate.
  • Connect real data through an API and cache it for offline use.
  • Add a print view for monthly reports.

5How it is built

Plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, with the map drawn in SVG; there is no build step or framework. The sample data comes from a seeded generator, so it is the same on every load. District boundaries are computed as Voronoi cells and district labels are placed automatically where they overlap the fewest schools.
